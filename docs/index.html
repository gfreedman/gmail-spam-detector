How it works

A time-driven Apps Script trigger fires every 15 minutes. Each run searches your entire inbox — Primary, Updates, Promotions, Social, and Forums tabs — for email that hasn't been checked yet. Each message is analyzed independently. Nothing changes until the verdict is in.

Step 1
Search

Finds all inbox email from the last 24 hours without a SpamChecked label. Covers every Gmail tab in one query.

{in:inbox category:updates category:promotions category:social category:forums} newer_than:1d -label:SpamChecked
Step 2
Analyze

Each message passes through 8 independent signal detectors — bulk infrastructure check, domain blacklist, clickbait patterns, Unicode obfuscation, fear language, marketing sender format, and more. Signals feed 6 rules evaluated in priority order. First match wins.

Step 3
Delete

Spam is reported to Gmail via the API — this trains Gmail's own filters — then immediately deleted by message ID using Gmail.Users.Messages.batchDelete. A safety-net sweep runs at the end of every cycle to catch anything that reached the spam folder through other paths.

Step 4  ·  automatic
Re-check

At the end of every run, recheckRecentSpamChecked() re-evaluates inbox email from the past 2 days that was previously cleared. If a pattern fix was just deployed and an email now scores as spam, it's caught and deleted within 15 minutes — no manual action required.

Rules

Six rules, evaluated in priority order. The first rule that matches wins — no further rules are checked. Rules 1–3 all require bulk email infrastructure as a prerequisite, which eliminates virtually all false positives from personal senders.

RuleConditionRationale
Rule 1 Bulk email + blacklisted sender domain Known spam mill domains combined with bulk infrastructure. Zero false-positive risk — these domains have no legitimate use.
Rule 2 Bulk email + 2+ clickbait patterns Legitimate bulk senders (GitHub, Stripe, your bank) almost never use multiple clickbait tactics simultaneously. Two independent pattern hits is a strong signal.
Rule 3 Bulk email + 2+ distinct spam behaviors Counts any clickbait, fear-mongering, marketing sender format, or suspicious From display name as a "behavior." Two unrelated spam indicators firing together is statistically anomalous.
Rule 4 3+ clickbait patterns (no bulk email required) Three clickbait pattern hits is so anomalous that even direct-send messages are almost certainly spam. Catches campaigns that avoid SES/SendGrid/Mailchimp.
Rule 5 Empty subject + attachment Legitimate email virtually never combines a blank subject with an attachment. This is the fingerprint of payload-delivery scams hiding phishing links inside Excel or PDF files.
Rule 6 Cloud service subject from non-service sender Phishing campaigns impersonating Google Docs or OneDrive are delivered via compromised accounts — not bulk infrastructure. The subject template alone is definitive: a real cloud service always sends from its own domain.

Signals

Eight independent signals feed the rules. Each signal answers a specific factual question about the email — bulk infrastructure? domain blacklisted? clickbait count? fear language? — without reference to the others. Verdict logic lives in the rules, not the signals.

📡
Bulk email service
Checks raw headers and body for fingerprints of Amazon SES, SendGrid, Mailchimp, and Iterable. Legitimate personal senders never route through these. Required for Rules 1–3.
🚫
Blacklisted sender
Substring match against a curated list of known spam mill domains — investorplace, financebuzz, budgetingjournals, economicrulebook, and others. Matched against the extracted email address only, not the display name, to prevent spoofing.
🎣
Clickbait count
How many distinct clickbait patterns fired on subject + From combined. Each pattern targets a category of spam tactic — shock language, celebrity name-dropping, financial doom, age-based fear — not specific words, which spammers rotate constantly.
🔡
Unicode obfuscation
Detects Cyrillic lookalikes (Еlоn), Greek substitutions (Βanks), fullwidth characters (＄2 Bill), and mathematical bold surrogates (𝗔𝗺𝗮𝘇𝗼𝗻) in both the subject and email body. Legitimate English email never uses these. A single hit is high-confidence.
💎
Crypto scam body patterns
High-confidence terms that almost never appear in legitimate email bodies: "airdrop" and "connect your wallet." Checked against the full body text, not just headers, because crypto scam copy is rarely in the subject line.
⚠️
Fear-mongering
Boolean: does the subject or From field contain fear language? IRS/FBI/government + threat verbs, bank account + seizure language, health + danger verbs, or standalone urgency words like BREAKING, URGENT, WARNING. One match is enough.
📢
Marketing sender format
Detects spammy From field patterns: "Name | Org" pipe separators, "Name at Organization" constructions, business names with investment/profit/insider/alert in them, and subdomain patterns like @F.FinanceInsiderPro.com.
🔍
Suspicious From display name
Flags display names longer than 50 characters (keyword stuffing) or containing bullet separators (•). Legitimate senders use plain names. Spam mills stuff headlines into the From field: "Breaking • Banks Closing • Act Now".

Patterns

The clickbait signal counts how many distinct pattern categories fire. Each pattern targets a spam tactic — not a specific phrase. Spammers rotate individual words constantly; what they can't change is the category of manipulation they're using.

😱
Shock & sensation
"shocking," "stunning," "bizarre," "mysterious," "leaked," "exposed," "forbidden," "bombshell" — high-arousal adjectives with no informational content.
🏛️
Celebrity / political
Named public figures paired with credibility verbs: "Trump warns," "Musk exposes," "RFK reveals." Separately catches endorsement framing: "Trump approved," "Biden backed." Separately catches coin/collectible scams.
📉
Financial doom
Market / money terms combined with crisis words: "dollar dying," "economy collapse," "gold alert," "stock plunge." Also catches "ripped off," "looted," "bilked" — political financial anger framing — and "payback time."
🤫
Conspiracy / suppression
"What they don't want you to know," "watch before this gets buried," "before they remove it," "read before it's censored." False urgency implying authority is hiding the content.
👴
Demographic targeting
Age-based fear: "seniors at risk," "if you're over 60," "born before 1960." Year-based urgency: "2025 warning," "2026 crisis." These targeting markers almost never appear in legitimate email subjects.
💊
Health anxiety
"fatigue," "blood sugar," "cholesterol," "brain fog," "belly fat," "inflammation," "joint pain" — health condition anxiety triggers common in supplement and alternative medicine spam.
💰
Financial hype
Pre-IPO solicitations, "$5 per share" / "$0.72/share" stock price notation, penny stocks, income opportunity lures ("passive income," "side income"), and "0% APR" / balance transfer pitches.
🪙
Collectible scams
"limited edition," "rare coin," "gold-plated," "silver-plated," "commemorative," "collector's edition" — often combined with political figures. The coin is never the point; the email list harvest is.
🔣
Unicode obfuscation
Cyrillic, Greek, fullwidth, and mathematical alphanumeric characters used as visual lookalikes for ASCII letters. Caught in both subject/From (clickbait signal) and email body (Signal 2c) separately.
📅
Date-format stuffing
Newsletter spam often embeds dates in subjects to appear timely: "• January 29 •" (bullet), "| February 23" (pipe), "[March 09]" (bracket), "- Mar 11, 2026" (dash). All four variants are covered.

Bulk email fingerprints

amazonses.com   x-ses-   sendgrid.net   mcsv.net   iterable.com

Fingerprints are substring-matched against the full raw email content including headers and body. Iterable's CDN appears in image URLs embedded in HTML — it doesn't need to be in headers to be detected.

Setup

Runs entirely inside Google Apps Script. No server, no external dependencies, no OAuth flow to maintain. Your email stays in your Google account.

Step 1
Clone the repo

You'll deploy using clasp, Google's CLI for Apps Script. Install it with npm.

git clone https://github.com/gfreedman/gmail-spam-detector
cd gmail-spam-detector
npm install -g @google/clasp
clasp login
Step 2
Create your Apps Script project

Creates a new script in your Google account and links it locally.

clasp create --type standalone --title "Gmail Spam Detector"
clasp push
Step 3
Run setup()

Open the script in your browser, select the setup function, and click Run. This creates the SpamChecked label in Gmail and initializes domain lists in Script Properties. Required once.

clasp open   # opens script.google.com
# Select setup() → Run
Step 4
Set the time trigger

In the Apps Script editor: Triggers → Add Trigger → processInbox → Time-driven → Minutes timer → Every 15 minutes. The trigger fires automatically from here on. No cron, no server.

Safety

The script permanently deletes email, which is irreversible. These safeguards are why false positives on the 17-email ham corpus are zero — and why they should stay there.

  • Every email is labeled SpamChecked before any action — prevents reprocessing the same message on the next run
  • Spam is reported to Gmail via GmailApp before deletion — trains Gmail's own Bayesian filter with your feedback
  • Rules 1–3 require bulk email infrastructure as a prerequisite — personal senders are structurally excluded from most rules
  • Domain whitelist bypasses detection entirely for known-good bulk senders (Substack, LinkedIn, your bank)
  • Per-message try/catch — one email that throws an error doesn't abort the run or affect other messages
  • 50 emails per run cap — prevents the Apps Script 6-minute execution timeout under heavy backlog
  • 5 MB size cap — emails over 5 MB are skipped rather than processing potentially problematic large payloads
  • LockService guard — concurrent trigger invocations skip rather than queue, preventing execution pile-up
  • Input sanitized to 100 KB before pattern matching — prevents ReDoS on adversarially crafted subjects
  • False negative auto-recheck — emails cleared in the last 2 days are re-scored on every run; pattern fixes take effect within 15 minutes of deploy
  • Spam intelligence log — every deletion is written to a Google Sheet and archived as a raw EML in Drive for audit and review

Building a Training Dataset

Pattern-based detection is precise and auditable, but hand-written rules hit a ceiling. The spam intelligence log isn't just an audit trail — it's a labeled training corpus that grows automatically with every run.

51 confirmed spam
17 curated ham
growing with every run

The signal object is already a feature vector

Every detection event logs 8 independent signals alongside the verdict:

{ isBulkEmail, clickbaitCount, hasFearLanguage,
  hasMarketingFormat, hasBlacklistedSender,
  hasFromAnomaly, hasBodyUnicode, hasBodyCrypto }

That 8-dimensional vector is the input a classifier needs. The rule-based system generates the label. High-precision rules produce high-precision training labels — the thing that matters most when training on a small corpus.

Spam and ham stay separate

Detected spam is archived under Spam Intelligence/Detected/ and logged as spam in the Sheet. The 17 ham examples in tests/ham_examples/ are a fixed hold-out set — never mixed into training — used only to validate that pattern changes don't create false positives. That separation maps directly to the train/test split you'd use in supervised learning.

False negatives are labeled too

Spam the script misses and you manually flag via the SpamMissed label is archived under Spam Intelligence/False Negatives/ with Log Type = FALSE_NEGATIVE. These are the hard examples — the ones that fooled the current rules — and they're the most valuable training signal for a future classifier.

Path to ML

At roughly 1,000 labeled examples, the corpus is large enough to train a lightweight classifier (logistic regression or gradient boosting on the 8-signal vector). The rule-based system would continue as a high-precision first pass; the classifier handles edge cases that require subtle combinations the rules don't cover. The intelligence log is already structured for that handoff.

FAQ

My bank, newsletter, or GitHub sends bulk email — will it get deleted?

No, if you whitelist it first. Run addToWhitelist('yourdomain.com') once in the Apps Script editor and that domain bypasses all detection rules permanently. Common high-volume senders — Substack, LinkedIn, GitHub, Stripe, PayPal, your bank's notification domain — are already in DEFAULT_DOMAINS.legitimate and are excluded by default without any configuration.

Why permanently delete instead of just moving to spam?

Two things happen in sequence. First, reportSpam() is called — this signals Gmail's Bayesian classifier the same way clicking "Report spam" in the UI does, training it across your account. Then the message is permanently deleted via the Gmail API. The reason for deletion rather than a folder move: Gmail's Spam folder doesn't auto-clear, particularly on Workspace accounts. Emails you've already positively identified as spam shouldn't consume storage, surface in search results, or require a second manual deletion.

Does it work with Google Workspace / corporate Gmail?

Yes. The script uses GmailApp and Gmail.Users.Messages, which behave identically across personal Gmail and Workspace accounts. One difference: your Workspace administrator may need to authorize the OAuth scopes (gmail.modify and gmail.labels) before the script can run under a managed account. If the authorization prompt fails or is missing scopes, check with your admin about allowing user-installed Apps Script projects.

What if it deletes something I wanted?

Run setupLogging() once from the Apps Script editor. After that, every deletion is written to a Google Sheet — timestamp, rule triggered, sender, subject, all 8 detection signals — and the full raw EML is archived in Google Drive under Spam Intelligence/Detected/. If an email disappears, open the Sheet, find the row, and download the EML from the linked Drive file. The signal columns also tell you exactly which patterns matched, which makes it straightforward to decide whether to whitelist a domain and prevent future catches.
